> ### Abstract
> **Purpose:** The combination of fulvestrant with alpelisib, a PI3K inhibitor, improves progression-free survival in metastatic hormone receptor-positive, *PIK3CA*-mutant breast cancer. This study describes the incidence, risk factors, and treatment of alpelisib-associated hyperglycemia.
>
> **Methods:** Patients with metastatic breast cancer who received alpelisib from 2013 to 2021 at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center were included in this retrospective study. Alpelisib prescription dates and patient/tumor characteristics were abstracted from medical records. Risk factors associated with hyperglycemia and alpelisib dose reduction/discontinuation were evaluated using Pearson's $\chi^2$ tests.
>
> **Results:** Among 247 patients, baseline median body mass index was 25.4 kg/m<sup>2</sup> and median hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) was 5.5%. A total of 152 patients (61.5%) developed any-grade hyperglycemia and 72 patients (29.2%) developed grade 3-4 hyperglycemia; median time to onset was 16 days. A total of 100 patients (40.5%) received alpelisib on a clinical trial; rates of hyperglycemia were significantly higher in patients treated as standard care versus on a clinical trial (any-grade hyperglycemia 80.3% vs. 34.0%, grade 3-4 hyperglycemia 40.2% vs. 13.0%, $p < .001$). Baseline HbA1c was significantly associated with development of hyperglycemia ($p < .001$) and alpelisib dose reduction/discontinuation ($p = .015$). Among those who developed hyperglycemia, 101 (40.9%) received treatment, most commonly with metformin. A total of 49 patients (19.8%) were referred to an endocrinologist, which was associated with SGLT2 inhibitor prescription ($p = .007$).
>
> **Conclusions:** Rates of hyperglycemia among patients treated with alpelisib as standard care were significantly higher than patients treated on clinical trials. Elevated baseline HbA1c is associated with alpelisib-induced hyperglycemia and requiring dose modification. Optimization of glycemic status before alpelisib initiation should become routine practice.
>

# INTRODUCTION

Breast cancer is the most common cancer among women in the United States and is a leading cause of cancer-related death.<sup>1</sup> Approximately 45% of hormone receptor (HR)-positive and 23% of human epidermal growth factor 2 (HER2)-positive breast cancers harbor mutations in *PIK3CA*.<sup>2–4</sup> The *PIK3CA* gene encodes the p110α catalytic subunit of PI3K, a protein that mediates insulin signaling and cell growth; cancer cells exploit this pathway to increase nutrient uptake resulting in uncontrolled proliferation and cell survival.<sup>5</sup> The SOLAR-1 phase 3 randomized controlled trial demonstrated a significant improvement in median progression-free survival (PFS) with alpelisib plus fulvestrant compared with fulvestrant alone (11.0 vs. 5.7 months; *p* < .001) in patients with tumors harboring *PIK3CA* mutations.<sup>6</sup> Median overall survival was numerically greater but not statistically significant at 39.3 months for patients treated with alpelisib and fulvestrant, and 31.4 months for fulvestrant alone.<sup>7</sup>

Hyperglycemia is an adverse effect of PI3K inhibitors that occurs frequently. PI3K inhibition impairs physiologic insulin signaling in the liver and skeletal muscle, leading to increased glycogen breakdown, decreased glucose uptake, and a state of insulin resistance; thus, hyperglycemia is an "on-target, off-tumor" effect.<sup>8,9</sup> Hyperglycemia triggers compensatory hyperinsulinemia, which has been shown in preclinical models to reactivate PI3K pathway signaling despite the presence of a PI3K inhibitor.<sup>10</sup> In the SOLAR-1 trial, 63.7% of alpelisib-treated patients experienced hyperglycemia of any grade and 36.6% experienced grade 3–4 hyperglycemia; the alpelisib discontinuation rate due to hyperglycemia was 6.3%.<sup>6</sup> Metformin was the first line anti-hyperglycemic agent used for any grade of hyperglycemia.<sup>11</sup>

Apart from the SOLAR-1 trial, reports describing PI3K inhibitor-induced hyperglycemia and its management in everyday practice are sparse. The objective of this study was to describe the incidence, risk factors, and treatment patterns of alpelisib-associated hyperglycemia in a cohort of patients with metastatic breast cancer treated on a clinical trial or as standard care at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSKCC).

# MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Study cohort

This is a retrospective cohort study of adult patients with metastatic breast cancer who received alpelisib (BYL-719) from January 1, 2013 to October 15, 2021 at MSKCC. Patients were identified for inclusion by alpelisib prescription dates. Individuals were excluded if a prescription was written but per the electronic medical record, they were never treated with alpelisib, if they were treated outside of MSKCC, or if they had a coexisting malignancy besides nonmelanoma skin cancers. This study was approved by the institutional review board of MSKCC.

## Patient characteristics

Alpelisib start and end dates were determined based on electronic prescribing records and dates were adjusted based on manual chart review (for example, alpelisib initiated *x* days after prescription start date due to insurance processing and medication shipping, or alpelisib held/discontinued before prescription end date). Demographic data and pretreatment body mass index (BMI), hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), and serum glucose levels were abstracted from medical records. BMI was categorized per standard definitions: underweight/normal weight <25 kg/m<sup>2</sup>, overweight 25–29.9 kg/m<sup>2</sup>, and obese ≥30 kg/m<sup>2</sup>. HbA1c was categorized per American Diabetes Association definitions: normal <5.7%, prediabetes 5.7%–6.4%, and diabetes ≥6.5%.<sup>12</sup> For baseline BMI and HbA1c values, the value closest to but before alpelisib start date was recorded. Tumor receptor status (estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor, and HER2 receptor), therapies administered in the neoadjuvant/adjuvant setting (if applicable), lines of treatment in the metastatic setting, concurrent endocrine therapy administered (if applicable), and whether alpelisib was administered as standard care versus in a clinical trial setting were recorded.

## Clinical end points

The highest glucose value and date following initiation of alpelisib that occurred during the treatment period was recorded and assigned hyperglycemia grade according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 4.0. Time to onset of hyperglycemia was defined as the number of days between alpelisib start date and first instance of glucose ≥140 mg/dL. Alpelisib dose interruptions, reductions, or discontinuation as well as endocrinology consultation and prescriptions of anti-hyperglycemic agents were recorded. Time to resolution of hyperglycemia was defined as the number of days between first anti-hyperglycemic agent start date and first instance of glucose <140 mg/dL. Alpelisib planned versus delivered doses were used to calculate relative dose intensity (RDI). Standard dose intensity (SDI) was calculated using 300 mg daily for standard care patients and the prescribed clinical trial dose for clinical trial patients, multiplied by duration of treatment in days. Delivered dose intensity (DDI) was calculated using actualized cumulative dose based on prescription changes over the same time period. For each patient, RDI was calculated using DDI divided by SDI, expressed as both mg per day and as a percentage. Date of cancer progression and/or death was recorded where applicable.

## Statistical analyses

The primary outcome in this study was rate of hyperglycemia. Secondary end points included hyperglycemia severity, median time to onset of hyperglycemia, clinical characteristics associated with hyperglycemia, use of anti-hyperglycemic medications, endocrinology consultation, and progression-free survival. # RESULTS

A total of 247 patients were included in this study, 147 (59.5%) of whom were treated with alpelisib as standard care and 100 (40.5%) of whom were treated on a clinical trial. Baseline patient and treatment characteristics are shown in Table 1 for the entire cohort and by treatment cohort (i.e., standard care vs. clinical trial); clinical trial details are shown in Table 2. Baseline BMI and HbA1c data were available for 235 (95.1%) and 164 (66.4%) patients, respectively. Compared to the clinical trial cohort, a greater proportion of patients in the standard care cohort had overweight/obese range BMI (55.7% vs. 48.0%, $p = .09$). Among all patients treated with alpelisib, median BMI change was $-1.30\text{ kg/m}^2$ ($-5.5\%$ of initial BMI) (interquartile range, $-0.33$ to $-3.0$); BMI change was not significantly different between the standard care and clinical trial cohorts ($p = .2$). A greater proportion of patients in the standard care cohort had HbA1c measured at baseline (72.1% vs. 58.0%, $p = .021$) and a greater proportion had HbA1c in the prediabetes/diabetes range of $\ge 5.7\%$ (30.6% vs. 15.0%, $p = .041$) compared to the clinical trial cohort. Alpelisib median RDI was 277 mg daily (92% of intended dose) in the standard care cohort versus 246 mg daily (99% of intended dose) in the clinical trial cohort ($p < .001$ for both comparisons).

In the overall population, hyperglycemia of any grade occurred in 152 patients (61.5%); 56 patients (22.7%) developed grade 3 and 16 patients (6.5%) developed grade 4 hyperglycemia. The median time to onset of hyperglycemia was 16 days. Rates of hyperglycemia were significantly higher in the standard care versus clinical trial cohort (Figure 1, $p < .001$). In the standard care cohort, the rate of any-grade hyperglycemia was 80.3%; 15 patients developed grade 1 hyperglycemia (10.2%), 45 developed grade 2 (30.6%), 43 developed grade 3 (29.3%), and 16 developed grade 4 (10.9%) (Figure 1). In the clinical trial cohort, the rate of any-grade hyperglycemia was 34.0%; 5 patients developed grade 1 (5.0%), 16 developed grade 2 (16.0%), 13 developed grade 3 (13.0%), and 0 developed grade 4 hyperglycemia. Baseline BMI, baseline HbA1c, and baseline glucose levels were all significantly associated with development of hyperglycemia ($p = .029$, $p < .001$, and $p < .001$, respectively). When analyzed as categorical variables, baseline BMI $\ge 25\text{ kg/m}^2$ was associated with development of any-grade hyperglycemia and grade 3–4 hyperglycemia ($p = .036$ and $p < .001$, respectively), and baseline HbA1c $\ge 5.7\%$ was associated with development of any-grade hyperglycemia and grade 3–4 hyperglycemia ($p < .001$ and $p < .001$, respectively). There was a significant interaction between baseline BMI and HbA1c ($p = .005$); when both variables were included in the model for association with development of hyperglycemia, only baseline HbA1c remained significant ($p < .001$). In a sensitivity analysis excluding patients who developed diabetic ketoacidosis and/or in the top 5% of blood glucose levels, the difference in hyperglycemia rate between standard care and clinical trial patients remained significant ($p < .001$), as did the association between baseline hemoglobin A1c and development of hyperglycemia ($p = .001$).

Among those who developed hyperglycemia ($n = 152$), 101 (66.4%) received anti-hyperglycemic treatment. Rate of anti-hyperglycemic agent use was similar between the standard care and clinical trial cohorts (68.6% vs. 61.8%). Overall, 69 patients (68.3%) required one anti-hyperglycemic agent, 23 (22.8%) required two anti-hyperglycemic agents, and nine (8.9%) required three or more anti-hyperglycemic agents. Among those who received hyperglycemia treatment, 90 (89.1%) patients received metformin either alone or in combination, 20 (19.8%) received a sodium glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitor, 16 (15.8%) received insulin, 12 (11.9%) received a dipeptidyl-peptidase 4 (DPP4) inhibitor, eight (7.9%) received a thiazolidinedione, and six (5.9%) received a sulfonylurea. The median time to resolution of hyperglycemia with metformin monotherapy was 16 days (interquartile range, 7–26). Only three patients received SGLT2 inhibitor monotherapy, and there was no significant difference in time to resolution of hyperglycemia compared to metformin monotherapy ($p = .5$). There was no significant difference in time to resolution of hyperglycemia with metformin versus other anti-hyperglycemic agent as initial hyperglycemia intervention ($p = .7$). For patients with persistent hyperglycemia, the median time to resolution of hyperglycemia if additional agents were combined with metformin was 26 days (interquartile range, 14–64), which was significantly longer compared to metformin monotherapy ($p = .024$). In total, 49 patients (19.8%) were referred to an endocrinologist for further management, which varied by cohort (30.0% in the standard care cohort vs. 6.0% in the clinical trial cohort, $p < .001$). Endocrinology consultation was associated with use of an SGLT2 inhibitor ($p = .007$).

In 66 patients (26.7%), alpelisib was held until resolution of hyperglycemia; 42 patients (17%) required dose reductions, and 11 (4.5%) discontinued alpelisib due to hyperglycemia. Dose reduction/discontinuation due to hyperglycemia occurred in 43 patients (29.3%) in the standard care cohort versus 10 patients (10.0%) in the clinical trial cohort ($p = .3$). BMI $\ge 25\text{ kg/m}^2$, HbA1c $\ge 5.7\%$, or referral to an endocrinologist were associated with alpelisib dose reduction/discontinuation ($p < .001$, $p = .015$, $p < .001$, respectively). Prescription of an SGLT2 inhibitor was also associated with alpelisib dose reduction/discontinuation ($p = .007$).

Two small studies have previously reported rates of hyperglycemia with alpelisib use. In a single-center study of 16 patients with metastatic breast cancer treated with alpelisib, the hyperglycemia rate was 56%, with a grade 3–4 hyperglycemia rate of 19%.<sup>13</sup> In a retrospective study of 62 patients with metastatic breast cancer treated with alpelisib, 64.5% developed any-grade hyperglycemia, 46.4% required alpelisib dose reduction, and 14.5% discontinued alpelisib due to hyperglycemia.<sup>14</sup> Several case reports have described an increased risk of severe hyperglycemia and diabetic ketoacidosis with alpelisib treatment.<sup>15–18</sup> In our study, the overall rate of hyperglycemia was similar to these reports, but we found that the rate of hyperglycemia was significantly higher in patients treated as standard care compared with those treated on a clinical trial.

Second, the clinical trials in which study participants were enrolled included phase 1 and 2 trials where doses of alpelisib lower than the standard 300 mg daily were used, as demonstrated by the difference in daily median RDI of 277 mg in the standard care cohort and 246 mg in the clinical trial cohort. To assess whether these differences in prescribed alpelisib dose contributed to the disparate hyperglycemia rates between cohorts, we performed a sensitivity analysis to compare hyperglycemia rates between the standard care cohort and the subgroup of patients in the clinical trial cohort that received the standard 300 mg daily dose ($n = 33$). The difference in hyperglycemia rates between cohorts remained significant (80% vs. 52%, $p < .001$). Finally, hyperglycemia monitoring and management were more structured and intensive in some clinical trial protocols versus clinical practice.

We identified baseline BMI, HbA1c, and blood glucose as risk factors for development of hyperglycemia, although there was a significant interaction between BMI and HbA1c such that only baseline HbA1c was independently predictive. Interestingly, pretreatment HbA1c was measured less frequently in our clinical trial cohort versus in standard care patients. This difference was largely driven by the 49 patients enrolled in NCT01870505, a phase 1 trial initiated in 2013, in which baseline HbA1c was not included in screening blood tests nor was it used as an exclusionary criterion.<sup>19</sup> It is likely that baseline HbA1c is now measured more frequently in clinical practice due to its inclusion in alpelisib labeling. However, even among standard care patients, only 72% of patients had baseline HbA1c levels available. Based on our findings, we strongly advocate for incorporation of baseline HbA1c measurement into routine clinical practice for patients who are candidates for PI3K inhibitor treatment. Early HbA1c measurement (i.e., at diagnosis of metastatic disease or during first-line treatment) would provide lead time for the implementation of lifestyle interventions such as the Diabetes Prevention Program<sup>20</sup> or early consultation with an endocrinologist to optimize metabolic risk factors before alpelisib initiation.

There were several differences in the use of anti-hyperglycemic agents in our cohort compared with the SOLAR-1 trial. The use of any anti-hyperglycemic medication was less frequent in our cohort (66%) than in the SOLAR-1 trial (87%). Consistent with the alpelisib label, metformin was the most common intervention in our cohort and in SOLAR-1, although fewer patients in our cohort required 3 or more anti-hyperglycemic agents and fewer received insulin. These differences may be related to the lower incidence of grade 3–4 hyperglycemia in our cohort. Additionally, nearly one-third of patients in our cohort consulted with an endocrinologist, which was associated with use of an SGLT2 inhibitor. Consultation with an endocrinologist and addition of an SGLT2 inhibitor to metformin were late interventions for persistent and higher-grade hyperglycemia, which likely accounts for the association of these management strategies with alpelisib dose reduction and/or discontinuation and longer time to resolution of hyperglycemia.

Our findings indicate the need for more effective strategies to mitigate PI3K inhibitor-associated hyperglycemia. More than half of patients in our study required additional anti-hyperglycemic agents beyond metformin. The primary mechanism of metformin action is activation of AMP-activated protein kinase, which is abolished by PI3K inhibition, although there may be some clinical efficacy at high doses.<sup>21,22</sup> Additionally, overlapping gastrointestinal adverse effects may limit metformin use and dose escalation with alpelisib.<sup>23–25</sup> Therefore, newer anti-hyperglycemia agents may be preferred. For example, SGLT2 inhibitors are a newer class of diabetic medications that inhibit renal glucose reabsorption. In preclinical studies, SGLT2 inhibitors successfully suppress PI3K inhibitor-induced hyperglycemia.<sup>10,26</sup> Clinically, several case reports have highlighted success in managing alpelisib-associated hyperglycemia with SGLT2 inhibitors; use of these agents is associated with greater reductions in serum glucose levels compared with metformin.<sup>27–29</sup> Dietary strategies may also be successful adjuncts to reducing hyperglycemia and may even contribute antitumor effects. In mice bearing orthotopic Kras-Tp53-Pdx-Cre tumor allografts in the pancreas, use of a very low carbohydrate (ketogenic) diet in conjunction with a PI3K inhibitor led to lower blood glucose levels, lower insulin levels, reduced PI3K signaling, smaller tumors, and prolonged survival compared with PI3K inhibitor alone or in combination with metformin.<sup>10</sup> In humans, the Targeting Insulin Feedback to Enhance Alpelisib (TIFA) trial is an ongoing phase 2 randomized control trial that is testing the efficacy of ketogenic and low carbohydrate diets and SGLT2 inhibitors for attenuating alpelisib-induced hyperglycemia (NCT05090358).<sup>30</sup> Finally, although insulin is still indicated for the management of severe hyperglycemia and/or ketoacidosis, it should be avoided when possible, as hyperinsulinemia can reactivate PI3K signaling despite use of a PI3K inhibitor.<sup>10</sup> Given the complexity of anti-hyperglycemic treatment considerations in this setting, multidisciplinary support including consultation with dieticians and endocrinologists is warranted.<sup>31,32</sup> Additionally, prospective studies to identify effective anti-hyperglycemic strategies during alpelisib treatment are underway, with four clinical trials actively recruiting patients (Table 3).

Reassuringly, there was no significant difference in PFS between patients who developed hyperglycemia and those who did not, nor did PFS differ by grade of hyperglycemia, baseline BMI, or hemoglobin A1c. These observations are consistent with findings from the SOLAR-1 trial: the PFS advantage in the PIK3CA-mutant group treated with alpelisib plus fulvestrant was consistent among patients with or without prediabetes or diabetes at baseline.<sup>11</sup> The recently reported CAPItello-291 study demonstrated that capivasertib, an AKT inhibitor, significantly prolonged PFS in combination with fulvestrant compared with placebo plus fulvestrant (7.2 vs. 3.6 months, hazard ratio, 0.60; 95% CI, 0.51–0.71); the all-grade hyperglycemia rate was 16.1% in the capivasertib arm.<sup>33</sup> These encouraging data present a

To our knowledge, this is the largest study describing PI3K inhibitor-associated hyperglycemia in a cohort of patients with metastatic breast cancer treated with alpelisib. However, these observations should be interpreted in the context of relevant limitations. First, serum glucose levels were abstracted from electronic records which did not differentiate fasting versus nonfasting status. Although obtaining fasted glucose levels is standard practice during alpelisib treatment at MSKCC, inadvertent nonfasted levels could have led to overestimation of hyperglycemia rates in the subgroup of patients treated as standard care. To address this potential limitation, we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding patients who developed diabetic ketoacidosis and/or in the top 5% of blood glucose levels, and all observed associations remained significant. Second, it is difficult to draw definitive conclusions regarding the effectiveness of various anti-hyperglycemic strategies, because subsequent improvements in serum glucose levels could be attributed to a variety of factors including dietary changes, alpelisib interruption and/or dose reductions, and sequential or simultaneous use of multiple anti-hyperglycemic medications. These limitations do not detract from the identification of overweight, obesity, and prediabetes as risk factors for hyperglycemia and alpelisib dose reduction or discontinuation. Finally, only 4.5% of patients in our cohort self-reported Black race. Rates of obesity and diabetes are higher among Black adults than other racial groups, and studies addressing this disparity in the context of alpelisib treatment are needed.<sup>34</sup>

In conclusion, alpelisib-associated hyperglycemia occurs at a higher rate in clinical practice versus clinical trials. Elevated baseline HbA1c is a significant predictor of the development of hyperglycemia and alpelisib dose modification due to hyperglycemia. Candidates for alpelisib treatment should be identified during early lines of treatment for metastatic disease, during which there is a critical window to improve glycemic status through lifestyle and pharmacologic interventions before initiation of alpelisib treatment. Earlier optimization of glycemic status could be particularly advantageous given the rapidity of hyperglycemia onset within 14–16 days of alpelisib initiation during which time this risk factor is no longer meaningfully actionable. Although this risk-mitigation approach requires earlier identification of PIK3CA mutations, it could ultimately improve PI3K inhibitor drug delivery and quality of life.
